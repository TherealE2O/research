## PART I: BIRTH & THE GEOPOLITICAL LABYRINTH OF JADA (1946)

### 1.1 The Birth of an Only Son
Atiku Abubakar was born on **November 25, 1946**, in **Jada**, a rural settlement situated in the undulating savannah highlands of what is today southern Adamawa State, Nigeria. He was named after his paternal grandfather, Atiku Abdulkadir. 

Atiku’s entry into the world was marked by heavy familial sorrow: an older sister had died in infancy prior to his birth, leaving him as the sole surviving child of his parents. In Fulani culture, an only surviving child is often regarded with an agonizing blend of desperate protectiveness and spiritual vulnerability, an identity that shaped young Atiku's upbringing and fostered a fierce, lifelong survival instinct.

However, the geographic and temporal coordinates of his birth—**Jada, November 25, 1946**—would, seven decades later, become the explosive evidentiary fulcrum of an unprecedented constitutional dispute regarding his eligibility to occupy the presidency of the Federal Republic of Nigeria.

### 1.2 The International Law Evolution of Jada
To comprehend the legal controversy that surfaced in 2019 and 2023, Jada’s intricate sovereignty history under international treaties must be forensically dissected:

1. **The German Kamerun Era (1884–1916):**
   Following the Berlin West Africa Conference of 1884–1885, Imperial Germany declared a protectorate over *Kamerun*. The territory of the historic Adamawa Emirate (Fombina), founded by Modibbo Adama during the early 19th-century Sokoto Jihad, was ruthlessly bifurcated by colonial drawing boards. The western part fell within the British Northern Nigeria Protectorate, while the larger eastern part—including Jada, Ganye, Toungo, and Mubi—fell within German Kamerun.

2. **World War I and the Allied Partition (1914–1916):**
   Following the outbreak of the First World War, joint British, French, and Belgian forces invaded German Kamerun. By February 1916, German forces surrendered. On March 4, 1916, Britain and France signed the **London Declaration**, dividing the occupied German territory into British and French administrative zones. The French took the lion's share (roughly 80%), while the British took two narrow, disconnected strips along the eastern border of Nigeria.

3. **The League of Nations Mandate (1922–1946):**
   Under Article 22 of the Covenant of the League of Nations, the British zones were officially designated as a **Class B Mandate** on July 20, 1922. The territory was formally christened the **British Cameroons**. Because the mandate consisted of two geographically separated land masses cut in two by the Benue River valley, the British colonial apparatus opted not to administer them as an autonomous colony. Instead:
   - **Southern Cameroons** (comprising Bamenda, Victoria, Kumba, and Mamfe) was attached to the Southern Provinces (later Eastern Region) of Nigeria.
   - **Northern Cameroons** (comprising Dikwa Emirate in the north, and the Adamawa/Gashaka-Mambilla enclaves, including Jada) was attached administratively to the Northern Provinces (later Northern Region) of Nigeria.

4. **The United Nations Trusteeship Agreement (December 13, 1946):**
   With the dissolution of the League of Nations after World War II, the United Nations General Assembly approved the **Trusteeship Agreement for the Territory of the Cameroons under British Administration** on December 13, 1946 (UN Document A/RES/63(I)). Under Article 5(a) of the Trusteeship Agreement, the Administering Authority (the United Kingdom) was granted full powers of administration, legislation, and jurisdiction:
   > *"The Administering Authority shall have full powers of legislation, administration and jurisdiction in the territory and shall administer it in accordance with the Authority's own laws as an integral part of its territory with such modifications as may be required by local conditions..."*

5. **The Temporal Intersection of Atiku's Birth:**
   Atiku Abubakar was born on **November 25, 1946**—precisely eighteen days *before* the formal UN Trusteeship resolution was enacted by the General Assembly, while the territory was in the legal transition between the League of Nations Mandate and the United Nations Trusteeship. Crucially, in November 1946, Jada was:
   - **NOT** part of the Colony of Lagos;
   - **NOT** part of the Protectorate of Northern Nigeria;
   - An international trust/mandate territory administered *alongside* Nigeria for administrative convenience, whose inhabitants were classified as **British Protected Persons (BPPs)**, distinct from British subjects or subjects of the Crown.

### 1.3 The 1959 and 1961 Plebiscites: Partitioning a Mandate
As Nigeria barreled toward full sovereign independence from Great Britain in 1960, the United Nations Trusteeship Council and General Assembly mandated that the populations of British Cameroons must decide their own political destiny pursuant to the right of self-determination enshrined in the UN Charter.

#### The 1959 Abortive Northern Plebiscite
In November 1959, the UN conducted a preliminary plebiscite in British Northern Cameroons, asking voters whether they wished to join the Northern Region of Nigeria upon its impending independence. In an unexpected rebuke to Sir Ahmadu Bello (the Premier of the Northern Region), the population voted 70,546 to 42,788 to defer a decision and remain under temporary British trusteeship. The local population was protesting against the high-handedness and perceived hegemony of the Native Authority officials dispatched from Maiduguri and Yola, demanding administrative reforms before committing to full political merger.

#### The Decisive February 11–12, 1961 UN Plebiscite
A second, definitive plebiscite was organized under the supervision of the United Nations Plebiscite Commissioner, Ambassador Abdoh of Iran, on **February 11 and 12, 1961**. Voters were presented with two unambiguous choices:
1. *Do you wish to achieve independence by joining the independent Federation of Nigeria?*
2. *Do you wish to achieve independence by joining the independent Republic of Cameroon?*

The outcome split the territory down its historic seam:
- **Northern Cameroons** decisively rejected unification with Yaoundé, voting by nearly 60% (146,296 votes) to permanently integrate with the Federation of Nigeria.
- **Southern Cameroons** overwhelmingly rejected Nigeria, voting by 70.5% (233,571 votes) to merge with the French-speaking Republic of Cameroon (a decision that sowed the seeds of the modern-day Anglophone Crisis / Ambazonia conflict).

#### UN General Assembly Resolution 1608 (XV) & Sardauna Province
On April 21, 1961, the United Nations General Assembly adopted **Resolution 1608 (XV)**, endorsing the plebiscite results and setting **June 1, 1961**, as the effective date for the termination of the Trusteeship Agreement over Northern Cameroons.

On **June 1, 1961**, Northern Cameroons was officially ceded to Nigeria. In honor of Sir Ahmadu Bello, the Premier of Northern Nigeria who had personally campaigned across the region, the newly incorporated strip was constituted into **Sardauna Province**, with Mubi as its provincial headquarters and Jada as one of its key administrative districts.

When Nigeria’s military regime under General Murtala Muhammed created 19 states in February 1976, Sardauna Province was dissolved into the newly created **Gongola State**. Later, on August 27, 1991, General Ibrahim Babangida split Gongola State into Taraba and **Adamawa State**, leaving Jada as the headquarters of Jada Local Government Area within Adamawa State.

## PART II: THE CITIZENSHIP CONTROVERSY & CONSTITUTIONAL JURISPRUDENCE

### 2.1 The Constitutional Trilemma: Section 131(a) vs Section 25
The core legal dispute raised by political adversaries against Atiku Abubakar in 2019 and 2023 centered on the strict qualification requirements for the Nigerian presidency.

Under **Section 131** of the 1999 Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria (as amended):
> *"A person shall be qualified for election to the office of President if:  
> (a) **he is a citizen of Nigeria by birth**;  
> (b) he has attained the age of thirty-five years;  
> (c) he is a member of a political party and is sponsored by that political party; and  
> (d) he has been educated up to at least School Certificate level or its equivalent."*

The crux is subsection (a): unlike legislators or ministers who may be citizens by registration or naturalization, a Nigerian President **MUST** be a *"citizen of Nigeria by birth."*

To define who qualifies as a "citizen by birth," the law turns to **Section 25(1)** of the 1999 Constitution:
> *"The following persons are citizens of Nigeria by birth, namely:  
> (a) every person born in Nigeria **before the date of independence**, either of whose parents or any of whose grandparents belongs or belonged to a community indigenous to Nigeria:  
> **Provided that a person shall not become a citizen of Nigeria by virtue of this section if neither of his parents nor any of his grandparents was born in Nigeria;**  
> (b) every person born in Nigeria after the date of independence either of whose parents or any of whose grandparents is a citizen of Nigeria; and  
> (c) every person born outside Nigeria either of whose parents is a citizen of Nigeria."*

### 2.2 The Missing Constitutional Link: Section 10 of the 1963 Constitution
The flaw in the argument advanced by Atiku’s opponents lay in their deliberate omission of the transitional constitutional mechanisms enacted when Northern Cameroons joined Nigeria.

Because Nigeria had already attained independence on **October 1, 1960**, while Northern Cameroons did not officially integrate until **June 1, 1961**, the British Parliament and the Nigerian Parliament had enacted specific legislative adaptations.

When Nigeria transitioned to a Republic, **Section 10 of the 1963 Constitution of the Federation (Act No. 20 of 1963)** explicitly codified the legal status of persons connected to the Northern Cameroons:

> **Section 10: Special Provisions as to Northern Cameroons**  
> *(1) For the purpose of determining the citizenship of any person who was connected with that part of Northern Nigeria that was not included in the Federation on the first day of October, 1960, but was integrated with the Federation on the first day of June, 1961 (hereinafter in this section referred to as "the Northern Cameroons"), the provisions of this Chapter shall have effect subject to the following modifications:  
> (a) any reference to the first day of October, 1960, shall be construed as a reference to the **first day of June, 1961**;  
> (b) any reference to the former Colony or Protectorate of Nigeria shall be construed as including a reference to the **Northern Cameroons**.*  
> *(2) Nothing in this section shall be construed as depriving any person of any status which he possessed immediately before the coming into operation of this Constitution.*

By operation of Section 10(1)(a) and (b):
1. For any individual connected to Northern Cameroons, the constitutional cutoff date of October 1, 1960, was legally read as **June 1, 1961**.
2. Any reference to the "Protectorate of Nigeria" in relation to ancestral birth was legally deemed to include **Northern Cameroons**.
3. Therefore, because Atiku was born in Northern Cameroons before June 1, 1961, and belonged to an indigenous community (the Fulani of the Adamawa basin), the law treated his birth and parentage as having occurred in the Protectorate of Nigeria prior to the effective date of independence.

When the 1979 Constitution was drafted by the Constitution Drafting Committee (CDC) and enacted under the Constituent Assembly, and subsequently re-enacted in the 1999 Constitution, the draftsman used the broad phrase *"a community indigenous to Nigeria."* It was never the legislative intention of the constitutional drafters to retrospectively disenfranchise or strip millions of Nigerians inhabiting Adamawa, Taraba, and Borno of their citizenship by birth.

### 2.3 The 2019 Presidential Election Petition Tribunal (CA/PEPC/001/2019)
Following the February 23, 2019 presidential election in which President Muhammadu Buhari was declared the winner by the Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC), Atiku Abubakar and the Peoples Democratic Party (PDP) filed Petition No. **CA/PEPC/001/2019** before the Presidential Election Petition Tribunal (PEPT) sitting at the Court of Appeal, Abuja.

#### The APC Preliminary Objection
In their joint reply and preliminary objection drafted by a team of Senior Advocates of Nigeria (including Lateef Fagbemi, Charles Edosomwan, and Alex Izinyon), the All Progressives Congress (APC) dropped a constitutional bombshell:
- The APC asserted that Atiku Abubakar lacked the *locus standi* to initiate or maintain an election petition challenging the outcome of the presidential election because he was not qualified to contest the election in the first place.
- The APC argued that Atiku was born in Jada in 1946; that Jada was not part of Nigeria until the plebiscite of June 1, 1961; and that under Section 131(a) and Section 25 of the 1999 Constitution, Atiku was a Cameroonian citizen by origin who became Nigerian merely by registration, naturalization, or administrative plebiscite, and thus was legally barred from aspiring to the presidency.

#### The PEPT Judgment (Delivered September 11, 2019)
The five-man panel of the PEPT, presided over by **Justice Mohammed Lawal Garba** (later elevated to the Supreme Court), with Justices Abdul Aboki, Joseph Ikyegh, Samuel Oseji, and Peter Ige, delivered its marathon judgment on **September 11, 2019**.

The Tribunal **dismissed the APC’s objection** on decisive procedural and jurisdictional grounds:
1. **Jurisdiction of the Election Tribunal:** The Tribunal held that under Section 239 of the 1999 Constitution and Section 138 of the Electoral Act 2010 (as amended), an election tribunal is empaneled to hear petitions challenging the election and return of a candidate declared winner. A respondent (the APC) cannot utilize its reply to challenge the qualification or citizenship of the *unsuccessful petitioner* unless it had filed a formal, independent cross-petition.
2. **Pre-Election Limitation Period:** The issue of candidate qualification is fundamentally a pre-election matter governed by **Section 285(14) of the 1999 Constitution (Fourth Alteration)**. Any person or party seeking to disqualify a candidate based on false information in his CF001 affidavit or constitutional disqualification must institute the suit at the **Federal High Court** within **14 days** from the date INEC publishes the candidate’s particulars. Having failed to invoke that constitutional mechanism within the mandatory 14-day window, the APC was completely estopped from reviving it collaterally at the election petition tribunal.
3. **The Territorially Integrated Status:** The Tribunal noted obiter that even on the substantive merits, treating persons from territories integrated through lawful international plebiscites as non-citizens by birth was legally untenable and contrary to the spirit of national unity and historical treaty succession.

#### The Supreme Court Appeal (SC.1211/2019)
Atiku Abubakar appealed the tribunal’s substantive dismissal of his petition (which had failed on claims of electronic server manipulation and widespread irregularities) to the Supreme Court. On **October 30, 2019**, a seven-man panel headed by the Chief Justice of Nigeria, **Justice Ibrahim Tanko Muhammad**, dismissed Atiku’s appeal in its entirety, affirming the tribunal's dismissal of the main petition. The APC's citizenship gambit was rendered legally moot at the apex court.

### 2.4 The EMA Litigation & Abubakar Malami's Astounding Submission
While the election petition tribunal was concluding, a parallel, highly coordinated legal offensive was launched in the regular courts.

#### *EMA v. Atiku Abubakar & 3 Ors* (Suit No: FHC/ABJ/CS/177/2019)
In February 2019, an incorporated NGO known as the **Incorporated Trustees of Egalitarian Mission for Africa (EMA)** filed an originating summons before the Federal High Court in Abuja, marked **FHC/ABJ/CS/177/2019**.
- **Defendants:** Atiku Abubakar (1st), Peoples Democratic Party (2nd), INEC (3rd), and the Attorney-General of the Federation (4th).
- **Reliefs Sought:** A declaration that Atiku is not a Nigerian citizen by birth; an injunction restraining INEC from ever placing his name on the ballot for the presidency; and an order disqualifying him from contesting the 2019 or any future presidential election.

#### The AGF's Extraordinary Stance (Abubakar Malami, SAN)
In an extraordinary political and legal maneuver, the 4th Defendant—the **Attorney-General of the Federation and Minister of Justice, Abubakar Malami (SAN)**, representing the Federal Government of Nigeria—filed a counter-affidavit deposed by a State Counsel, **Fabian Adechy**, aggressively siding with the plaintiff against the former Vice President!

In the AGF’s written address, Malami submitted that:
> *"The 1st Defendant [Atiku] was born on November 25, 1946, in Jada, Adamawa State, in the then Northern Cameroon. He was not born in Nigeria. Atiku acquired Nigerian citizenship by virtue of the 1961 plebiscite. By virtue of this historical fact, the 1st Defendant is not a citizen of Nigeria by birth and is therefore constitutionally ineligible to contest the office of President of Nigeria."*

The spectacle of a sitting Attorney-General of the Federation formally asserting in open court that an entire geopolitical belt of millions of Nigerians (including Adamawa, Borno, and Taraba indigenes) were second-class citizens ineligible for the presidency ignited furious outrage across the northern intelligentsia and civil society.

#### The Judgment of Justice Inyang Ekwo (February 21, 2022)
On **February 21, 2022**, **Justice Inyang Eden Ekwo** of the Federal High Court, Court 5, Abuja, delivered a blistering judgment dismissing the EMA lawsuit:
1. **Lacks Locus Standi:** Justice Ekwo held that the plaintiff, EMA, completely lacked the legal capacity (*locus standi*) to institute the action. The court ruled that EMA, being registered under Part C of the Companies and Allied Matters Act (CAMA) with specific educational, cultural, and charitable objectives, possessed no legal mandate or sufficient interest to meddle in public electoral eligibility disputes.
2. **"Busybody and Meddlesome Interloper":** Justice Ekwo denounced the NGO as a *"busybody and meddlesome interloper"*, warning civil society organizations against weaponizing the judicial system for political vendettas:
   > *"The plaintiff has no locus standi to maintain this action. Public interest litigation is not a licence for busybodies and meddlesome interlopers to poke their noses into matters where they have no personal or direct civil right or obligation to protect. This suit is an abuse of judicial process and is hereby dismissed."*

### 3.1 Paternal Lineage: Garba Atiku Abdulkadir
Atiku’s father, **Garba Atiku Abdulkadir**, was an itinerant Fulani trader, cattle dealer, and itinerant Islamic mallam. His ancestral lineage originated in **Wurno**, a historic political and spiritual redoubt in present-day Sokoto State (founded by Sultan Muhammad Bello as the secondary capital of the Sokoto Caliphate). 

Garba’s father, Atiku Abdulkadir, had migrated eastward from Sokoto following traditional pastoral and trade routes, eventually settling in the fertile grasslands of the Adamawa Emirate under the suzerainty of the Lamido of Adamawa. Garba inherited this mobile lifestyle: he was a devout, ascetic Muslim who traveled constantly between Jada, Toungo, Ganye, and Mayo Belwa, trading hides, livestock, kolanuts, and textile wares.

Garba was intensely conservative, deeply suspicious of Western colonial institutions, and fiercely hostile to the spreading influence of British Christian missions and English-language education, which he viewed as a covert vehicle to dismantle Islamic values and Fulani customs.

### 3.2 The Enrolment Conflict of January 1954: Imprisonment over Schooling
In early 1954, an administrative crisis occurred in Jada that would alter the trajectory of Nigerian history:

1. **The Secret Registration:** In January 1954, Atiku's maternal uncle, **Kawa Ali**, recognized the shifting political winds across the Northern Region, where Sir Ahmadu Bello was calling for the education of northern children. While Garba was away on a protracted trading journey, Kawa Ali secretly enrolled the seven-year-old Atiku into **Jada Primary School**.
2. **The Father's Violent Reaction:** When Garba returned and discovered his only surviving son dressed in Western school shorts and carrying an English slate, he was apoplectic. To Garba, Western schooling (*boko*) was an existential blasphemy. He immediately withdrew Atiku from the school, publicly vowing that his son would never sit under the tutelage of Christian-influenced colonial educators.
3. **Arrest and Alkali Trial:** Under the Northern Native Authority laws enacted to combat high illiteracy, withholding a registered child from primary education was a criminal misdemeanor. The local Native Authority administration moved swiftly. Garba was arrested by the *Dogarai* (Native Authority police) and dragged before the local **Alkali Court** in Jada.
4. **The Fine and Imprisonment:** The Alkali judge found Garba guilty of defying the educational mandate and sentenced him to pay a punitive fine of **10 shillings**, or serve a custodial jail sentence. Garba, both unable and ideologically unwilling to pay a single penny to validate Western schooling, refused. He was promptly marched into the Native Authority prison in Jada, where he was locked behind bars for several days.
5. **The Grandmother's Rescue:** The standoff was broken by young Atiku’s maternal grandmother, **Inna**. Heartbroken at seeing her son-in-law languishing in prison and recognizing the immense social shame attached to the incarceration, she mobilized her meager resources. Inna spent days boiling ash lye, tallow, and animal fats to produce cakes of indigenous, handmade laundry soap. She hauled the soap to the rural village markets, selling them bar by bar until she successfully accumulated the **10 shillings**. She marched to the court treasury, paid the fine, and secured Garba’s release. 

Garba reluctantly capitulated to the Native Authority decree, and young Atiku returned to Jada Primary School.

### 3.3 Maternal Sanctuary: Aisha Kande
Atiku’s mother, **Aisha Kande**, provided the stable, nurturing counterweight to Garba’s itinerant volatility. Aisha’s family hailed originally from **Dutse** (in present-day Jigawa State). Her father, a merchant, had migrated south-eastward to Adamawa.

Aisha was a steadfast, deeply religious woman who shielded young Atiku from the harsh deprivations of rural poverty. Following her husband's early death, she never remarried, dedicating her life entirely to her only surviving son. She lived to witness Atiku’s meteoric rise through the ranks of the Department of Customs and Excise, his transition into lucrative private business, and his emergence as a wealthy political heavyweight. Aisha Kande passed away in **1984**, remaining Atiku’s most revered moral compass.

### 3.4 The Fatal Tragedy of Late 1957: Drowning in the Mayo Choncha River
In late 1957, when Atiku was only eleven years old, catastrophe struck the family.

Garba Atiku had embarked on an extensive trading excursion to **Toungo**, a border outpost south of Jada. Heavy late-season rains had battered the region, causing the river networks across the Adamawa highlands to swell into turbulent, lethal rapids. 

While attempting to return home to Jada, Garba reached the banks of the **Mayo Choncha river**. Local boatmen and villagers warned travelers that the river was at peak flood tide, with treacherous undertows and submerged debris. Impatient to return to his family and trusting his physical stamina, Garba attempted to ford the raging waters.

The roaring current overpowered him. Garba was swept off his feet and dragged into the deep torrents. Despite frantic shouts from onlookers on the riverbanks, he disappeared beneath the surface and drowned. 

His lifeless body was recovered several miles downstream by local fishermen after the waters subsided. He was buried near the riverbanks in Toungo according to Islamic rites.

#### The Psychological Imprint: The "Orphan Mentality"
The death of his father in 1957 transformed Atiku into an orphan and an only child at age eleven. This profound isolation left an indelible psychological scar. Throughout his memoirs and speeches, Atiku has repeatedly reflected on the acute terror of having no siblings, no safety net, and no father to shield him from the world. 

Sociologists and political biographers trace Atiku’s relentless, hyper-ambitious drive—his pursuit of vast material wealth, his construction of a massive 28-child family empire, and his obsessive decades-long quest for the Nigerian presidency—directly to this childhood trauma: the desperate psychological need to ensure that he and his lineage would never again be vulnerable, helpless, or erased.
